From safety stock to spendable cash

A warehouse keeps extra parts while it waits for a delivery. A supplier keeps extra cash while it waits to be paid. The same formula explains both and shows what faster, tokenized payments could change.

Why a warehouse keeps spare stock

Imagine a parts warehouse that uses about 100 units a day. Its next shipment is four days away, so it plans for 400 units of demand while it waits. Some days are busier than expected. A little extra stock covers those surprises.

Suppose the typical size of a daily demand surprise is 20 units, measured as a standard deviation. Use a safety factor of 2 to set the cushion. With a fixed four-day wait and independent daily surprises, the extra stock is 2 × 20 × √4 = 80 units. If the shipment can arrive in one day, the extra stock falls to 40 units. The warehouse still plans for the regular demand in either case. [1]

Why the square root? A busy day can be followed by a quiet one. With independent surprises, the squared measures of daily uncertainty add up. Four days give you four times the variance. Taking the square root brings you back to units of stock, so the size of the cushion only doubles.

Cash has a waiting period too

Think of a customer payment as a delivery of cash. A supplier uses its available cash to pay wages, freight, and other bills while that payment is on the way. It budgets for expected outflows and holds a cash buffer for surprises. That is the same planning problem the warehouse faces. Apply the same calculation to errors in the forecast of cash going out minus other cash coming in. [2]

SB=zσT
SB
Cash buffer. Extra cash for unexpected needs during the wait.
z
Safety factor. A larger value gives more protection.
σ
Forecast uncertainty. The standard deviation of net cash-outflow forecast errors in one period.
T
Funding window. How many periods the supplier must cover before it can use the incoming money.

Use the same unit of time for σ and T. If your forecast errors are measured over one minute, count the wait in minutes. Better forecasts can reduce σ. A faster, reliable payout can reduce T. The example below changes just the wait.

Goods still need to be made, loaded, and delivered. A payment can move as soon as its conditions are met. That makes the time it takes to get from an approved invoice to usable funds a variable that payment design can change.

Follow a Canadian buyer’s payment

A Canadian buyer owes a U.S. supplier US$100,000 at 10:00 a.m. ET. At an assumed rate of C$1.35 per US$1, the buyer’s payment provider needs to exchange C$135,000 for US$100,000. Each currency transfer is one leg of the exchange.

The parties use payment versus payment. The buyer’s provider sends CAD to the foreign exchange (FX) provider. The FX provider sends USD back. Each transfer becomes final only if the other one does too. The supplier’s payout is the next step. [3]

Where tokenization enters

A tokenized deposit represents a claim on a bank in a form that can move on a programmable payment platform. A shared rule can tell that platform to release the payout once the approved invoice is due, required checks are complete, and both currency transfers are final. The potential time saving comes from handling those steps together and triggering payout as soon as the funds are ready. The BIS’s Project Agorá prototyped linked currency settlement and conditional payments using tokenized bank deposits and central bank reserves. [4]

Start the supplier’s clock when the invoice is due. Stop it when the supplier can spend the full USD amount. If the supplier has to convert the tokenized balance into spendable bank dollars, or wait for a hold to clear, that time counts too. That is the T in this example.

What 28 minutes mean for the buffer

In this hypothetical case, the FX exchange settles at 10:00 a.m. in both routes. The first route has a payout handoff, and the USD becomes spendable at 10:30. In the tokenized route, a shared rule triggers the payout and the USD becomes spendable at 10:02. For a supplier whose cash needs are exposed during that wait, T falls from 30 minutes to 2.

Hold z and σ steady, so both cancel when you divide the new buffer by the old one.

When the estimate fits. It assumes a reliable funding window and independent forecast errors with the same variance each minute. If delays jump at cutoffs, outflows cluster, or the supplier already has cash to make every payment that falls in the window, use the supplier’s actual cash-flow and payout records to judge the effect.

Who needs cash, and for how long?

The example tracks two different balances. The supplier’s buffer covers uncertain spending before the invoice money arrives. The transaction’s peak funding is the largest amount that the buyer and FX provider must reserve at the same time to carry out the payment. It includes C$135,000 on the buyer side and US$100,000 at the FX provider, worth another C$135,000 at the assumed rate.

If the faster route instead requires the FX provider to reserve another US$20,000 as a liquidity margin, the provider commits an extra C$27,000 and the transaction peaks at C$297,000. The supplier’s estimated buffer can still be smaller. The extra reserve affects a different balance and may be held for a different amount of time.

Work through the C$297,000 case

One CAD-hour means one Canadian dollar tied up for one hour. Suppose both routes reserve their FX funds for an hour before 10:00 a.m., any extra margin is released when FX settles, and only the US$100,000 payout remains reserved until it becomes spendable. Convert that payout to C$135,000 using the example’s rate.

What would show a real benefit?

FedNow already settles domestic U.S. payments in seconds. Project Meridian FX showed that different payment systems could coordinate a currency exchange. If an existing route reliably delivers spendable USD in two minutes at the same funding level, the formula gives it the same supplier buffer. [5] [6]

To test a tokenized workflow, compare similar invoices and record when each one is due, when both currencies settle, and when the supplier can spend the proceeds. Include late and failed payouts. Track when each participant sets cash aside and when it gets that cash back. For example, if the supplier has a freight bill due at 10:15, the 10:02 payout lets it use the incoming USD to pay that bill. Check whether earlier access lets it avoid drawing on a credit line or keeping as much spare cash.

A balance that becomes spendable sooner can free up cash for the supplier’s next bill.
